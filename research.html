Research

Finding better porous materials, faster

From gas capture and separation today to catalysis next: simulation, physics-informed machine learning and stability predictions working as one discovery loop.

Metal–organic frameworks and related porous materials can be assembled from building blocks in millions of combinations. That makes them ideal for separating and storing gases, and increasingly attractive as catalysts. It also makes them impossible to explore one by one: only a small fraction can be simulated, and far fewer can be made.

My work builds the pieces needed to search this space efficiently: representations that encode the physics of adsorption, learning strategies that need little data, and stability predictions that keep the search anchored to materials that can exist.

  • RepresentPhysics-informed descriptors of host–guest interactions
  • LearnActive, transfer and multi-fidelity machine learning
  • ValidateThermodynamic stability, solvent effects, synthesis knowledge from the literature
01

Representations that carry physics

What does a model need to know about a pore to predict what it will adsorb?

Most machine-learning models for porous materials describe geometry: pore size, surface area, atom types. Adsorption, however, is governed by how a guest molecule interacts with the framework. I develop representations built from that physics, such as two-dimensional histograms of interaction parameters that summarize how strongly, and how often, a framework can interact with guest molecules.

Because they come from the same quantities used in molecular simulation, these representations are simple to compute, apply across very different materials and adsorbates, and give models a physically meaningful starting point.

02

Learning from less data

How few simulations does it take to map a whole design space?

A single adsorption simulation is affordable; screening thousands of materials across many gases, pressures and temperatures is not. I use active learning to decide which simulations are worth running, and transfer learning to reuse what a model learned on one property or molecule for another.

With alchemical adsorption simulations, one model can learn across families of “virtual” guest molecules, a step toward a universal adsorption model. In hierarchical screening workflows, iterative machine learning focuses expensive calculations on the most promising candidates.

03

From predicted to makeable

Of all the materials a model likes, which ones can actually exist?

A record-setting adsorbent is only useful if it can be synthesized and remains stable. I compute free energies of metal–organic frameworks to quantify thermodynamic stability, and study how functional groups and synthesis solvents shift it, including which polymorph is likely to form. Machine learning then predicts these free energies in a fraction of the time.

To connect predictions with how materials are really made, we use expert-guided large language models to extract synthesis procedures, step by step, from the literature.

Toward catalysis

If we can predict what a pore binds, can we predict what it transforms?

Adsorption is the first step of every catalytic cycle, and the confinement that makes a pore selective also shapes reactivity. The next stage of my work extends the discovery loop to catalytic materials: descriptors of active sites in their pore environment, machine-learned potentials that describe binding beyond physisorption, and the same emphasis on data efficiency and on stability under reaction conditions.

Earlier collaborations already point in this direction: computational screening of MOFs for plasma-assisted green ammonia synthesis, and a first-principles look at how the Pd–PdO phase transformation changes H₂O₂ synthesis and decomposition pathways.
